Chapter 1: Introduction

Ch 1 / 5 · Introduction Chapter Overview

Not just a compliance endeavour

Cybersecurity and resilience are strategic imperatives. This briefing equips senior leaders with a practical framework for understanding threats, making smarter trade-offs, and building genuine organisational resilience.

We will start this introduction chapter covering the following:

  • Assign Accountability At Every Level — different responsibilities at each level of leadership
  • Balance These Three — the three pillars every security decision revolves around
  • Build For Resilience — keeping services running when defences are breached
  • Understand The Three Types of Threats — categorising threats by preventability

Assign Accountability At Every Level

Cybersecurity and Resilience is not the sole domain of IT teams. It is a shared responsibility that spans every level of government leadership.

Balance These Three

Every cybersecurity decision revolves around three pillars: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability, known collectively as the CIA Triad.

  • Confidentiality ensures information remains accessible only to those authorised to see it.
  • Integrity guarantees that data has not been tampered with, so what appears on screen is accurate and trustworthy.
  • Availability ensures systems and data remain accessible when needed.

📖 Dictionary

Cybersecurity — The measures and practices used to protect systems, networks, and data from attacks

Build For Resilience

Cybersecurity keeps threats at bay. Resilience ensures the government can keep operating even when those defences are breached.

The distinction matters. Many government services are now delivered digitally, and citizens expect them to work reliably, around the clock. Resilience goes beyond deploying security controls. It encompasses business continuity planning, disaster recovery, and the ability to restore services quickly when disruptions inevitably occur.

📖 Dictionary

Business continuity planning — Preparing in advance a set of actions, processes, and critical information that an organisation needs to continue operating during an unplanned event

Disaster recovery — Restoring systems and data after a major disruption

Understand The Three Types of Threats

A Framework for Understanding Threats

Different attackers have different intent and capabilities. To make sense of the landscape, it helps to categorise them by how preventable they are and who is behind them.

At the base of the threat pyramid sit hacktivists, whose attacks are largely preventable. One level up, lower-tier APTs pose threats that are largely preventable. At the apex, top-tier APTs, typically state-sponsored, execute attacks that are difficult to prevent.

📖 Dictionary

Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs) — Highly skilled, often state-sponsored attackers

Chapter 2: Preventable

Ch 2 / 5 · Preventable Threats Chapter Overview

Chapter 1: Preventable

This chapter covers what 'preventable' looks like in practice:

  • A. DDoS Attacks — how they work
  • B. DDoS Defences — cost-effective tools available to any agency
  • C. Not Every Service Outage Begins with an Attacker — Inadequate Planning, Misconfigurations, and Human Errors

Takeaway: Get the operational basics right.

DDoS Attacks

A server, at its core, is a computer with finite resources (CPU, memory, database connections, network connections, etc.). A Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attack works by flooding the server with so many requests that it crashes. Each time a server responds to a request, it uses up some of its resources. When requests exceed available resources, the server slows down dramatically and eventually crashes entirely.

DDoS attacks can happen at different layers of a system. At the network layer, attackers perform what is called a connection flood, using botnets to open millions of simultaneous connections and exhaust networking resources. At the application layer, botnets send requests that require resource-intensive processing, e.g. uploading large files repeatedly to hog application sessions.

An Attack Anyone Can Buy

What makes DDoS attacks especially dangerous is their accessibility. "DDoS-as-a-Service" platforms have turned cyberattacks into a cheap commodity.

Some plans start as low as €5 per month. A disgruntled individual, a politically motivated group, or a minor criminal can now launch an attack that brings down a government service, with no technical expertise whatsoever.

📖 Dictionary

DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service) — Flooding a server with so many requests it can't respond to real users

Botnet — A network of compromised devices controlled remotely to carry out attacks

Network layer / Application layer — Network layer = the connections and bandwidth; Application layer = the software and services running on top

DDoS-as-a-Service — Ready-made attacks sold on platforms

DDoS Defences

The good news: defending against DDoS is neither expensive nor complex.

DDoS Protection Services, typically bundled with CDNs such as Cloudflare and Akamai, act as a shield between attackers and servers. When attack traffic arrives, the service filters out malicious requests and forwards only legitimate traffic. Given that a majority of web traffic today is served through CDNs, it is established practice for a properly configured CDN to protect websites against some common malicious attacks like DDoS.

Firewalls add a second layer of defense, limiting the system's attack surface by whitelisting certain traffic and potentially blocking malicious outgoing traffic. Together, these two layers form a cost-effective, battle-tested defence architecture.

📖 Dictionary

CDN (Content Delivery Network) — A network of servers spread across the globe that stores copies of website content closer to where users are, so pages load faster

Firewall — Software or hardware that filters traffic based on rules, blocking what shouldn't get through

Not Every Service Outage Begins with an Attacker

A significant share of disruptions stem from inadequate planning, misconfigurations, and human errors.

A digital certificate expires because no one tracked the renewal date, or it was marked as not needing one. Traffic limits are not configured appropriately and sit on default settings. A failover plan exists on paper but has never been tested. A deployment goes live without proper checks.

None of these require a sophisticated adversary, yet each can take down critical services just as effectively as a cyberattack.

📖 Dictionary

Misconfiguration — A setting left at its default or set up incorrectly, e.g. (1) a database left open to the public internet because access controls were never configured, (2) a firewall rule that permits all traffic instead of only what is needed, or (3) leaving default passwords unchanged

Takeaway #1: Get the Operational Basics Right

These disruptions are preventable. The measures are neither complex nor expensive:

  • Deploy DDoS Protection Services and firewalls for all public-facing systems.
  • Build processes to reduce human error: checklists, peer reviews, automated safeguards, and change management controls.
  • Monitor and maintain: track certificate renewals, review default configurations, and ensure operational hygiene does not slip between audits.

Chapter 3: Largely Preventable

Ch 3 / 5 · Largely Preventable Chapter Overview

Chapter 2: Largely Preventable

This chapter covers largely preventable types of incidents:

  • A. Ransomware — how more methodical attackers (lower-tier APTs) exploit known and fixable gaps
  • B. Overly Complex Systems — how tangled architectures create hidden failure points
  • C. The Fix for Largely Preventable Threats

Takeaway: Make deliberate choices.

Ransomware

One tier above hacktivists sit lower-tier APTs. These attackers also exploit poor cyber hygiene, but are more methodical in their ways. Their weapon of choice is ransomware: malicious software that encrypts an organisation's data, rendering it inaccessible until a ransom is paid.

Attackers scan for unpatched systems, weak or default passwords, and environments lacking multi-factor authentication. Once inside, they deploy ransomware, lock critical files, and issue a financial demand. Some groups now operate "double extortion" schemes, threatening to leak stolen data publicly if payment is not made.

These attacks are more sophisticated than basic DDoS, but they remain largely preventable. The vulnerabilities they exploit are known. The defences are proven. What is often lacking is the discipline to implement them consistently.

📖 Dictionary

Ransomware — Malware that locks your files. Attacker unlocks when you pay a ransom

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) — Requiring two or more forms of ID to log in — e.g. password + phone code

Overly Complex Systems

Overly complex system designs are another source of largely preventable disruptions. Unknown dependencies and tangled architectures create conditions where unexpected failures cascade in ways that no one predicted, including the teams managing them.

The remedy begins with designing systems for simplicity. But where simplicity is not possible, outcome-based security thinking becomes critical. A compliance checklist will confirm that baseline requirements have been ticked off, but it will not reveal the hidden dependency that triggers a cascading failure at 2 a.m. Secure architecting and VAPT will.

Where complexity cannot be reduced, it must be rigorously tested.

📖 Dictionary

VAPT (Vulnerability Assessment & Penetration Testing) — Proactively testing systems for weaknesses before attackers find them

The Fix for Largely Preventable Threats

You Can't Outsource Accountability to Vendors

Cybercriminals do not always attack their target directly. Increasingly, they reach organisations through the supply chain, breaching a vendor with weaker defences to gain access to the ultimate target's data and systems.

Outsourcing IT infrastructure, cloud services, or data processing does not outsource accountability. When a vendor is breached, it is the client's data that is compromised, the client's customers who are affected, and the client's reputation that suffers.

  • Develop contingency plans for scenarios in which vendors are compromised.
  • Embed security requirements into contracts from day one, covering annual VAPT, incident notification timelines, and minimum security certifications.

Non-digital Alternatives

Your contingency plans can include non-digital fallbacks, which can be just as effective as a digital fallback.

During election polling, officials pivoted to manual processing using hardcopy polling station registers. When immigration clearance systems went down, ICA staff maintained low-tech alternatives, using iPads to keep queues moving.

These are not improvised workarounds. They are deliberate, rehearsed contingency measures that ensure service delivery continues even when digital systems do not.

Takeaway #2: Make Deliberate Choices

The threats in this category demand deliberate strategic decisions:

  • Be conscious of vendor risk exposure: recognise the residual risk of vendors getting hacked.
  • Design for simplicity. Simpler systems are more secure, more resilient, and easier to test.
  • Conduct VAPT on both internal and vendor systems. Test whether controls actually hold up.
  • Plan for continuity with non-digital fallbacks: deliberate, documented, and regularly rehearsed.

Chapter 4: Difficult to Prevent

Ch 4 / 5 · Difficult to Prevent Chapter Overview

Chapter 3: Difficult to Prevent

This chapter covers three categories where even the world's most well-defended organisations have been caught off guard:

  • A. Top-Tier APTs — state-sponsored actors with the resources to breach even Big Tech
  • B. Zero Days — exploits targeting unknown vulnerabilities that bypass all conventional defences
  • C. Cloud Service Failures — disruptions that originate from the very infrastructure you depend on
  • D. The Fix for Difficult to Prevent Threats

Takeaway: Some disruptions are beyond our control. How we respond matters more.

Top-Tier Advanced Persistent Threats

At the apex of the threat pyramid sit Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs), typically state-sponsored actors with the resources, patience, and expertise to compromise even the most well-defended networks.

They are called "persistent" for a reason. If one attack vector fails, they try another. They may spend months, sometimes years, inside a network before executing their objectives. Their toolkit includes zero-day vulnerabilities, custom-built malware, and sophisticated social engineering.

Unlike lower-tier APTs that are primarily motivated by financial gain, top-tier APTs pursue strategic intelligence objectives. They are not opportunistic. They are surgical.

📖 Dictionary

Zero-day vulnerability — A software flaw unknown to the vendor, so there's no patch yet

Social engineering — Manipulating people (not systems) to gain access e.g. impersonation, phishing etc.

Zero Days

A zero-day exploit targets a flaw that no one in the world knows about — not the software vendor, not the security community. Because the flaw is unknown, no patch exists. Developing or acquiring these exploits is extraordinarily expensive, and only top-tier APTs have the resources to do so.

Some of these exploits require no user interaction at all. No phishing email to click, no malicious link to follow. The attack executes silently, invisibly, and with devastating effect.

Objectives of top-tier APTs

Because these exploits are so costly, top-tier APTs are selective about where they deploy them. Their objectives typically fall into two categories:

  • Secrets: intelligence related to national security, defence, and critical negotiations.
  • Disruptions: seizing control of Critical Information Infrastructures to destabilise essential services.

For most organisations, there is a reassuring flipside: the cost of a top-tier attack means most will never be targeted.

Even Big Tech Falls

But when top-tier APTs do strike, even the most resourced organisations are vulnerable.

In 2023, a China-based threat actor tracked as APT Storm-0558 breached Microsoft — a company that spends billions on security and employs thousands of cybersecurity professionals — gaining unauthorised access to email accounts across approximately 25 organisations, including government agencies.

No organisation, regardless of its resources, is immune.

📖 Dictionary

Zero-day exploit — An attack that weaponises a zero-day vulnerability before any patch exists

ROI (Return on Investment) — Whether the value gained justifies the cost

Cloud Service Failures

Another difficult to prevent disruption that is beyond our control: cloud service failures. These originate from non-cyber causes — hardware faults, software bugs, misconfigurations — yet can be just as damaging as any cyberattack.

In February 2023, Microsoft Azure experienced a cascading power supply failure, causing widespread outages globally. In July 2024, CrowdStrike pushed a faulty system update that crashed millions of devices, bringing airlines, hospitals, and entire industries to a standstill.

Neither incident involved a cyberattack. Both caused disruptions on a scale that most organisations had not planned for.

📖 Dictionary

Cascading failure — One system failing triggers failures in others, like dominoes

The Fix for Difficult to Prevent Threats

Defence-in-Depth

Even if an attacker exploits a zero-day vulnerability to gain initial access, you want multiple layers of defence to contain and detect the breach.

  • Network segmentation limits how far an attacker can move once inside, containing the blast radius of a breach.
  • Application whitelisting ensures only approved software can execute, blocking malicious payloads even if they bypass initial defences.
  • Behavioural-based detection identifies suspicious activity by monitoring for anomalies rather than relying on known attack signatures.

Air-Gap What Truly Matters

For information that is truly secret, one of the most effective protections remains the simplest in concept: air-gapping. A network that is physically isolated from the internet cannot be remotely hacked, at least not through conventional means.

But air-gapped networks are costly to build and maintain. The starting point is a clear-eyed assessment: what information is genuinely secret, and what is merely sensitive? Not everything warrants the operational overhead of an air-gapped environment.

Business Continuity Plans

Business Continuity Plans are not optional. Digital services need well-planned, regularly rehearsed, non-digital service recovery procedures.

📖 Dictionary

Defence-in-depth — A security strategy that layers multiple defences so that if one fails, others still protect the system

Air-gapping — Physically disconnecting a system from the internet so it can't be remotely compromised

Business continuity planning — Preparing in advance a set of actions, processes, and critical information that an organisation needs to continue operating during an unplanned event

Takeaway #3: Some Disruptions are Beyond Our Control. How We Respond Matters More.

We need to build our muscle for how we respond to the unexpected. Additionally, we need to:

  • Practice defence-in-depth
  • Air-gapped networks for genuinely secret information. Air-gapping is costly and must be used selectively.
  • Ensure all digital services have business continuity plans

The goal is not to prevent the unpreventable. It is to ensure that when disruption arrives, we can continue operating.

Chapter 5: Mindset Shifts

Ch 5 / 5 · Mindset Shifts Chapter Overview

Three Mindset Shifts for Better Cybersecurity

Beyond technical controls, three shifts in how leaders think about risk can fundamentally improve an organisation's security posture:

  • Shift 1: Trade-off Trilemma — consciously choosing between security, cost, and usability
  • Shift 2: From Compliance to Outcomes — testing whether your defences actually work
  • Shift 3: Assume Breach. Assume Fail. — building for the world where defences have already been penetrated

Shift #1: The Trade-off Trilemma

Every cybersecurity decision involves a tension between three competing goals: Security and Resilience, Cost Savings, and Usability.

Maximising all three simultaneously is not possible. For systems handling highly confidential data, security must take precedence. For services where availability is paramount, resilience investments are non-negotiable.

The critical task for leaders is to make these trade-offs consciously rather than allowing them to be made by default.

Stick to Your Trade-offs

Many organisations start with strong security intent. But then the pressure mounts. Users complain. Stakeholders push for exceptions. Deadlines force shortcuts. Gradually, security measures get loosened.

The result is the worst of both worlds: neither a great user experience nor strong security.

This happens when trade-offs are not made consciously and documented upfront.

The fix is simple: make the trade-off, document the rationale, and commit. If circumstances change, revisit the decision deliberately, not reactively.

The Exponential Cost of Over-Insuring

The relationship between prevention and cost is not linear. It grows exponentially. Defending against the most common threats is relatively affordable. But as organisations attempt to guard against increasingly rare failures, costs escalate dramatically.

Achieving resilience against a single cloud provider's outage might require a multi-cloud architecture, which can run 2× to 10× the cost.

"100% prevention" is not a viable strategy. The goal is to invest proportionally: spending the most on threats that are both likely and impactful, and accepting residual risk for the rest.

🏋️ Crown Jewels Exercise

Take 10 minutes to consider two questions:

  1. What are your organisation's "crown jewels": the most critical assets, data, and systems? What would cause the greatest harm if compromised or unavailable?
  2. What support would help you better manage these trade-offs? More budget? Better tools? Executive support to push back on exception requests?

Shift #2: From Compliance to Outcomes

Too many senior leaders still treat cybersecurity as a compliance issue. We need to move from a checklist mentality to a risk-based, outcomes-driven approach.

Put simply: risk-based is deciding where to invest; outcomes-based is deciding how to verify.

  1. Step 1: Understand the risk. What data or Government service does the system handle? What is at risk when there is a cyberattack?
  2. Step 2: Design mitigations based on identified risks, not from a template.
  3. Step 3: Test for outcomes. Ask, "do our security controls actually work when tested?"

📖 Dictionary

Risk-based approach — Allocating security resources based on likelihood and impact of threats, not a fixed checklist

Outcomes-based approach — Verifying security controls actually work, not just that they're in place

Shift #3: Assume Breach. Assume Fail.

The third mindset shift is perhaps the most uncomfortable.

Plan for the possibility that your defences have already been breached, and that your resilience measures will eventually fail.

Accept that no defence is impenetrable and no system is infallible.

The Restaurant That Never Closes

How does "assume breach, assume fail" look like for resilience?

Consider a restaurant. Preventing a kitchen fire requires fire-safe equipment and trained staff — that is the equivalent of cybersecurity controls. But if we assume things will go wrong, prevention is not enough. Fire alarms detect problems early. Sprinklers limit damage. A backup stove allows cooking to resume. These are the detection, response, and recovery capabilities that keep the restaurant operating even when prevention fails.

Government digital systems need the same layered approach.

Summary

1. Cybersecurity and resilience are strategic imperatives.

2. These imperatives do not rest solely on IT teams. It is a shared responsibility that spans every level of government leadership.

3. Three categories of threats define the landscape: preventable, largely preventable, and difficult to prevent. We need to make honest assessments of what can and cannot be prevented.

4. Three mindset shifts determine whether an organisation is truly prepared:

  1. Shift 1: Make deliberate, upfront trade-offs (cost, usability, and security/resilience) and commit to them.
  2. Shift 2: Move from compliance to outcomes. Test whether defences work, not just whether they exist.
  3. Shift 3: Assume breach, assume fail. Build for the world where defences have been penetrated and your system is down. How will you ensure service continuity in such a scenario?

Perfect security is impossible. Significant improvement is within reach.
